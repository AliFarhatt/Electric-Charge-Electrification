Electric charge · essentials

Positive, negative and neutral objects

+

Positive object

Has a deficit of electrons. It became positive because electrons were removed.

−

Negative object

Has an excess of electrons. It became negative because electrons were added.

0

Neutral object

Has equal amounts of positive and negative charge, so the net charge is zero.

The key particle
In ordinary charging problems, electrons move. Protons remain bound inside the nuclei of atoms.
Quantity of electric charge

The formula Q = N·e

Q = N·e
Q = quantity of charge in coulombs (C) · N = number of excess or missing electrons · e = elementary charge
Elementary charge
e = 1.6 × 10−19 C

The magnitude of the charge of one electron or one proton.

Rearranged form
N = |Q| / e

Use the magnitude |Q| to calculate how many electrons are involved, then use the sign of Q to decide excess or deficit.

SituationCharge signElectron description
Electrons addedQ < 0Excess electrons
Electrons removedQ > 0Deficit of electrons
Calculation method

How to solve any Q = N·e question

  1. Identify what is given: Q or N.
  2. Convert the charge to coulombs if it is given in nC or μC.
  3. Use Q = N·e or N = |Q|/e.
  4. Use the electron information to determine the sign: gain electrons → negative, lose electrons → positive.
  5. Check that N is a whole number when the question is about an exact number of elementary charges.
nano
1 nC = 10−9 C
micro
1 μC = 10−6 C
elementary
e = 1.6 × 10−19 C

Modes of electrification

The three ways an object can be electrified

ModeDoes touching occur?Main ideaWhat happens to electrons?
Friction (rubbing)Yes, while rubbingTwo materials are rubbed together.Electrons transfer from one material to the other.
Contact / conductionYesA charged object touches another object.Electrons transfer through the point of contact.

Fast distinction
  • Friction: rubbing.
  • Contact: touching with a charged object.
  • Induction: the charged object stays nearby but does not touch.

Mode 3

Electrification by induction

Induction: a charged object is brought near a conductor without direct contact, causing the conductor’s electrons to rearrange.
With grounding

A conductor can be permanently charged by induction when it is also grounded. Grounding provides a path for electrons to move between the conductor and Earth.

  1. Bring the charged rod near the neutral conductor.
  2. Charges separate inside the conductor.
  3. Connect the conductor to ground.
  4. Remove the ground connection while the rod is still nearby.
  5. Then remove the charged rod.
Grounding

What grounding does

Definition
Grounding connects a conductor to Earth so electrons can move between the object and the Earth.
Negative rod near conductor

The rod repels electrons. When the conductor is grounded, some electrons can leave to Earth. If the ground is removed first, the conductor is left positively charged after the rod is removed.

Positive rod near conductor

The rod attracts electrons. With grounding, electrons can come from Earth into the conductor. Removing the ground first leaves the conductor with excess electrons, so it becomes negative.

Order matters in induction
  • Keep the charged rod nearby while grounding.
  • Remove the ground first.
  • Remove the charged rod last.
Electroscope

What an electroscope does

Purpose

An electroscope is an instrument used to detect whether an object is charged.

Neutral electroscope: leaves stay close together.
Charged or polarized electroscope: the leaves can diverge because they carry like charges and repel each other.
Electroscope reasoning

Why do the leaves separate?

Negative rod approaches

The negative rod repels free electrons in the electroscope away from the knob and toward the leaves.

Both leaves gain extra electrons, so both become negative. Like charges repel, so the leaves move apart.

Positive rod approaches

The positive rod attracts free electrons toward the knob.

The leaves are left with a deficit of electrons, so both become positive. Like charges repel, so the leaves move apart.

Very important
The rod does not need to touch the electroscope for the leaves to diverge. A nearby charged rod can cause induction / separation of charge.

Common mistakes

Mistakes that cost easy quiz marks

Avoid these
  • Saying a positive object has “extra protons.” In ordinary electrification, the relevant change is a loss of electrons.
  • Forgetting the sign: gains electrons → negative; loses electrons → positive.
  • Using nC or μC directly in Q = N·e without first converting to coulombs.
  • Getting a non-integer value of N and accepting it as an exact number of electrons.
  • Calling induction “contact.” In induction, the charging object is nearby but not touching.
  • Saying electroscope leaves attract. They diverge because they have the same sign and repel.
  • In induction with grounding, removing the rod before the ground. The ground must be removed first.
Worksheet practice · Set A

Quantity of charge — from the AMER worksheet

Original worksheet numbering is preserved. The conservation-of-charge part of Question 2 is omitted because it is outside the quiz scope.

Worksheet 1 · Q1

An object carries 7 elementary positive charges and 3 elementary negative charges. Find the net charge, including its sign.

Net elementary charges = 7 − 3 = 4 positive charges.
Q = 4(1.6 × 10−19) = +6.4 × 10−19 C.
Worksheet 1 · Q2(a)

A neutral object has 30 protons and 30 electrons. It loses 5 electrons. What is its new charge?

Losing electrons makes the object positive. It has a deficit of 5 electrons.
Q = +5e = 5(1.6 × 10−19) = +8.0 × 10−19 C.
Worksheet 1 · Q4

An object carries a charge of −4.8 × 10−19 C. Does it have an excess or deficit of electrons, and how many?

The negative sign means excess electrons.
N = |Q|/e = (4.8 × 10−19)/(1.6 × 10−19) = 3 excess electrons.
Worksheet 1 · Q5

How many electrons must be added to a neutral metal sphere to give it a charge magnitude of 1.0 nC? State the final sign.

1.0 nC = 1.0 × 10−9 C.
N = |Q|/e = (1.0 × 10−9)/(1.6 × 10−19) = 6.25 × 109 electrons.
Because electrons are added, the sphere is negative.
Worksheet practice · Set B

More Q = N·e calculations

Worksheet 1 · Q6

A small sphere has a charge of −2.00 nC. (a) How many excess electrons does it carry? (b) By what factor is this different from the number for −1.00 nC?

|Q| = 2.00 × 10−9 C.
N = (2.00 × 10−9)/(1.6 × 10−19) = 1.25 × 1010 excess electrons.
For −1.00 nC, the number is half as large, so the factor is 2.
Worksheet 1 · Q7

A system contains 212 electrons and 165 protons. (a) Find its total charge. (b) How many electrons must be added or removed to make it neutral?

There are 212 − 165 = 47 excess electrons.
Q = −47e = −47(1.6 × 10−19) = −7.52 × 10−18 C.
To become neutral, 47 electrons must be removed.
Worksheet 1 · Q8

A metal sphere carries −3.2 × 10−6 μC. (a) How many excess electrons are on it? (b) If me = 9.11 × 10−31 kg, find the total mass of those excess electrons.

First convert: −3.2 × 10−6 μC = −3.2 × 10−12 C.
N = (3.2 × 10−12)/(1.6 × 10−19) = 2.0 × 107 excess electrons.
Mass = N me = (2.0 × 107)(9.11 × 10−31) = 1.822 × 10−23 kg, an extremely small mass.
Worksheet 1 · Q10

The pulled tape carries 0.14 μC of positive charge per centimetre. What length corresponds to the transfer of 1.8 × 1013 electrons? Express the answer in metres and state the pulled tape’s sign.

Transferred charge magnitude: Q = Ne = (1.8 × 1013)(1.6 × 10−19) = 2.88 × 10−6 C = 2.88 μC.
Length = 2.88 / 0.14 = 20.57 cm = 0.206 m (approximately). The pulled tape is positive.

MCQ · Q1

Which statement is correct? A) positive when gaining electrons · B) negative when gaining electrons · C) positive when gaining protons · D) negative when losing electrons

B. Gaining electrons gives an object an excess of negative charge, so it becomes negatively charged.
MCQ · Q2

A neutral object gains 5.0 × 1012 electrons. Find its total charge.

Q = −Ne = −(5.0 × 1012)(1.6 × 10−19) = −8.0 × 10−7 C.
MCQ · Q3

An object has charge +4.8 × 10−19 C. How many electrons were transferred, and what happened to them?

N = (4.8 × 10−19)/(1.6 × 10−19) = 3. A positive charge means electrons were lost. Three electrons were lost.
MCQ · Q4

A small sphere has charge −3.2 × 10−18 C. How many excess electrons does it have?

N = (3.2 × 10−18)/(1.6 × 10−19) = 20 excess electrons.
Worksheet practice · Set D

Multiple choice — continued

MCQ · Q5

A neutral metal sphere loses 2.5 × 1013 electrons. What is the resulting charge?

Losing electrons makes it positive.
Q = +(2.5 × 1013)(1.6 × 10−19) = +4.0 × 10−6 C.
MCQ · Q6

An object initially has −6.4 × 10−19 C and then loses 7 electrons. Find its final charge.

Initial charge = −4e. Losing 7 electrons changes the charge by +7e.
Final charge = (−4e + 7e) = +3e = +4.8 × 10−19 C.
MCQ · Q7

Can an object have a charge of −3.0 × 10−19 C if charge is quantized in units of e = 1.6 × 10−19 C?

N = |Q|/e = 3.0/1.6 = 1.875, which is not an integer number of electrons. Therefore the stated charge is not possible as an exact isolated-object charge in this model.
Final summary

Everything to memorize

Core ideas
  • Electric charge can be positive, negative or zero.
  • A negative object has excess electrons.
  • A positive object has a deficit of electrons.
  • The quantity of charge is found using Q = N·e.
  • e = 1.6 × 10−19 C.
Electrification & detection
  • Friction = rubbing.
  • Contact = touching.
  • Induction = nearby charged object, no touching.
  • Grounding lets electrons move to or from Earth.
  • An electroscope detects charge; leaves diverge because like charges repel.
Gain electrons → negative  |  Lose electrons → positive
